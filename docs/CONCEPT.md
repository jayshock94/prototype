# Glizzy Lovers — concept recommendation

Status: proposal for Jay's review, 2026-09-30, revision 2. Nothing here is decided until the PRD (`PRD.md`) is signed off.

How this was produced: four sourced research sweeps (what makes games fun, the friendslop genre and browser party games, mobile-web multiplayer tech, name and IP), a citation-verification pass, six independent concept pitches written from different angles, three comparative judges with different rubrics, a synthesis, a critic pass, and a second round of three reviewers on the written docs. All of it was done by AI agents. The judges' scores are structured opinions, not measurements. Raw material is in `research/PRINCIPLES.md` and `research/PITCHES.md`.

**Evidence labels.** `[verified]` = confirmed from a live page or registry this session. `[unverified]` = a real, well-known source, or one seen in search excerpts, whose page could not be opened here (the sandbox blocked most fetches). `[panel judgment]` = a design or engineering opinion, not a research finding. Do not quote an `[unverified]` number publicly without re-reading the primary source.

## 2. Recommendation: Glizzy Lovers, The Grand Delivery

**Pitch.** Four friends ride rented unicorns roped to one wobbly cart. On the cart is the Grand Glizzy, a parade-float-sized hot dog with a face who is terrified of everything. Haul him from the grill to the picnic line before the bun goes soggy, while raccoons steal toppings, sit on the cart, spook the unicorns and wear your hat. Because every rope pulls the same object, moving *is* the co-op: pull together and the cart glides, pull apart and it twangs, spins, tips, and the hot dog screams. One thumb steers, one tap bonks. Join by four-letter code, no account, phone or laptop.

**Why it won** `[panel judgment]`. Interdependence is the movement verb rather than a bolted-on mechanic, which is what the co-op research rewards. Both of Jay's fantasy pillars are load-bearing: unicorns are the engine, raccoons are the whole comedy engine. Its silhouette (four unicorns roped to a screaming hot dog) reads in a ten-second clip. The pitch claimed a shorter schedule than the others; after correction it costs about the same, so schedule is not a reason to pick it. The feasibility judge's dissent, that the whole game rests on one shared physics object driven by four continuous inputs over phone networks, is the reason the plan builds nothing else until a measurable physics gate passes.

### The loop

- **First 30 seconds.** Open the link, type a name (blank gets a pun), tap Ride. You're on a unicorn in a parking-lot lobby that is already a toy: ride, honk, bonk hats off friends. Drag anywhere to steer (a floating stick appears under the thumb; your rope is tied to the cart, so steering is pulling). Tap to bonk the nearest raccoon (auto-aim, big radius, hit-stop, stiff faint with tongue out). Hold and release for a boost that yanks the cart. Every rider's pull direction and rope tension is drawn as a public ring, so the person pulling the wrong way is visible to everyone.
- **One delivery (3 to 5 minutes).** Haul the cart along one route segment with one fork (Safe: longer and calmer; Spicy: shorter, more raccoons, bonus Relish). Raccoons: Grabber (lurks until nobody faces it, snatches a topping, taunts), Chonk (sits on the cart and adds a puller's worth of weight), Spooker (screams; every unicorn in earshot bucks and hats land on the wrong heads), Hat Thief (steals your hat and wears it until bonked). Honks, boosts and spills feed an Alarm meter; when it fills, every raccoon on the route wakes and the last stretch is a getaway (the Swarm). A rider knocked off lands as a passenger on the nearest teammate's unicorn (pillion) and can still grab spilled toppings and bonk until re-hitched. Cross the picnic line: confetti, delivery graded on toppings intact and time to spare. Then a 20-second Topping Stop: equal-sized contribution icons per player (pulls, bonks, toppings saved, revives, hats recovered), a slow-motion replay of the delivery's biggest tip on everyone's screen with a blame button, a blooper title per player computed from telemetry ("Wrong-Way Puller"), and a fork vote.
- **A session (a Cookout Run, roughly 10 to 16 minutes).** Three deliveries, one per route segment; the Grillmaster's quota rises each time; the third adds the Bin Baron, a boss that takes two riders to shove aside. Late friends trot in from the screen edge mid-delivery and auto-hitch (the cart lurches). Pass: Overtime and a "one more?" prompt. Fail: a one-to-five star cookout review, a firing letter assembled from the run's stats ("you lost 11 toppings, 2 hats and, briefly, the unicorn"), and everyone wears a PROBATION name tag on the retry. Nothing persistent is ever lost.
- **Headcount rule.** Cart mass, raccoon cap and quota scale linearly with hitched riders and are re-evaluated on every seat change. A rider whose 60-second seat hold expires is unhitched and the cart lightens; a rider who quits is the same. The Bin Baron needs two riders when two or more are present, otherwise one rider plus a honk. Whether two players need their own tuning is then measured, not debated.
- **Meta.** Relish earned per delivery buys hats, tops, unicorn coats and manes at fixed, visible prices; no randomness anywhere. A Topping Sticker Book that starts at 2 of 12; a Raccoon Field Guide; some stickers only exist for rooms of four or more; some unlocks are earned by failing (a Lifeguard hat after the crew loses 20 toppings to the pond). The Hat Thief that took your hat persists as a named nemesis on the lobby Wanted Board until your crew gets it back. Creator, collections and outfits are in v0 but arrive last, as palette swaps and layered pieces; bespoke outfits, horn sounds, emote packs and cart decorations are v1. An optional username and password syncs everything across devices; it is offered once after the first delivery, and a "Save your hat" button stays in the lobby forever after so a player who said no can opt in any time. Post-launch: three or four named drops (new route, new raccoon, cosmetic set), not a live service.

### Ingredients from the other five pitches that are being kept

- From Cookout Run: wipeout-to-pillion, and automatic pillion when a phone goes dark, so the friend who takes a call becomes a joke and a helper instead of an anchor. Stickers only earnable in rooms of four or more.
- From Night Shift: the public pull-direction ring; the Alarm meter that guarantees a climax on the final stretch; telemetry-driven blooper titles; unicorn appetite (an unattended cart at a Topping Stop gets its toppings eaten); the nemesis Wanted Board.
- From Backyard Siege: equal-size per-player contribution icons on every delivery card; mid-delivery drop-in; rules that teach through a laugh (a raccoon that grabs a spilled topping eats it and leaves fatter, so spills feed the enemy); raccoon aggro weighted toward whoever bonks most, so the strong player draws the heat.
- From Raccoon Rush: the firing letter built from stats, PROBATION tags, the bureaucratic boss; failure-tied unlocks.
- From Cookout Cover-Up: Spooker screams launch hats onto the wrong heads; tuning numbers exposed as host settings.

Rejected for v1: a flick-toss gesture (the gesture set is already the feasibility judge's top complaint), spectator and audience modes, crew-level persistent collections, six players as a hard requirement (it's a test, not a promise), and a double-tap honk (it delays every bonk by a recognition window).

### Controls and platform

Three canvas gestures: drag (steer), tap (bonk), hold-release (boost). Five small buttons bottom-right: honk, hitch/unhitch, emote wheel with ping inside, THAT (marks the last ten seconds as a clip), and boost as a tap alternative to hold-release for players who can't hold a touch. A tap resolves on release within 300 ms, and a hold past 300 ms shows a visible boost charge, so tapping has no recognition delay `[panel judgment]`. Whether five buttons is too many is a playtest question.

The one-thumb rule, stated plainly: steering means the thumb is down for most of a delivery. With one thumb, bonking means releasing the pull for that instant, and your tension ring drops to zero while you do it. That is a deliberate co-op cost, and it is what makes the Grabber's "lurks until nobody faces it" logic bite. With two thumbs, a tap with the second finger anywhere bonks without releasing. Both are supported; which is the default is decided at Gate A.

Portrait-first because iPhone Safari cannot lock landscape `[unverified, widely reported]`, one-handed play works standing at an actual cookout, and the route runs up the screen so progress is legible. Camera locked on the cart so everyone sees the same thing and callouts work; it zooms out as ropes spread. Desktop is the same client rendering the same world through a wider 16:9 camera: WASD or arrows steer, Space bonks, Shift boosts, E hitches, Q honks, mouse click also bonks. A desktop-only room is a Gate C requirement in the PRD, and the wider view gives the laptop player more warning of incoming raccoons, which is as far as a "spotter role" goes in v0.

## 3. Ranked alternatives, and when to pick them instead

- **Backyard Siege (7.0; 5 to 6 months).** The richest raccoon reaction table on the panel and fully playable solo and offline. Lost because it is three games stacked (cooking stations, lanes, a mount layer) with twelve verbs, the largest content bill, and a portrait field that the feasibility judge calculated does not fit on a 375-pixel-wide phone `[panel judgment]`. Pick it if the rope gate fails and Jay wants clear roles and strong solo play.
- **Raccoon Rush (6.8; 4 to 5 months).** Overcooked for one thumb. **This is the designated fallback** if the shared-cart physics cannot be made to feel right on phones: it keeps unicorns, raccoons and the walkie-talkie boss. The reason it is the fallback `[panel judgment]`: every verb is a tap on a target resolved by the server, with no continuous input to feel laggy, so it tolerates a fixed delay; the panel guessed 250 ms, which is a guess until measured. Lost because station busywork competes with the comedy and the riding fantasy is rationed to one seat.
- **Night Shift (6.3; 5 to 6 months).** Second on friendslop feel; lost because stealth back-loads the fun (a quiet first minute), needs a joystick-deflection scheme that is fragile under a thumb, and is a one-to-one skeleton of Lethal Company. Pick it if Jay's group are committed Lethal Company players who want that exact rhythm.
- **Cookout Run (6.3; 3 to 4 months).** Won feasibility (input model native to phones, lightest netcode, 2 to 8 players) and lost both experience lenses: parallel play in a racing frame that reads competition-first. Pick it if the priority becomes a solo-capable, portal-friendly mobile game over a friend game, or if the budget is under three months.
- **Cookout Cover-Up (4.8; 4 to 5 months).** The single best comedic tell on the panel (the tail pops out when Trash Urge peaks), and it lost hardest: dead at two or three players, competitive among friends, and social deduction is a crowded space. Only if Jay reliably has six to eight people online and wants a party game rather than a co-op.

## 4. The research filter

Research is a filter, not a generator. Nothing below says whether four unicorns roped to a hot dog is funny. It rules out bad ideas and tells us what to measure. The hit, if there is one, comes from a tight core loop iterated with playtests until strangers laugh in a silent room.

Principles this design is built on, with what implements them:

1. **Competence, autonomy and relatedness each predict enjoyment and intent to keep playing.** Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski 2006, *Motivation and Emotion* `[unverified; correlational]`. Implemented: per-player contribution icons at equal size (competence); Safe/Spicy fork and hitch-or-chase (autonomy); the shared cart and pillion revive (relatedness by construction).
2. **Cooperation and interdependence raise relatedness, enjoyment and trust.** Depping and Mandryk 2017, CHI PLAY `[unverified]`. Implemented: the rope. One rider can't move the cart at full speed; boosts only launch it when synchronized; the Bin Baron needs two.
3. **Competition declines with age and is linked to aggression in lab studies.** Quantic Foundry 2016 survey and Emmerich and Masuch 2013 `[both unverified]`. "Co-op is the safer default for adult friends" is the panel's design choice from that. Implemented: co-op against raccoons is the only mode; in-team comparison is limited to affectionate blame and telemetry titles.
4. **Flow needs clear goals, immediate feedback and challenge matched to skill; let players pick difficulty implicitly.** Chen 2007, CACM `[unverified; an essay]`. Implemented: fork votes, aggro weighted to the strongest bonker, headcount scaling, host-exposed tuning numbers, no popups mid-delivery.
5. **Endowed progress: a head start increases persistence.** Nunes and Drèze 2006, *Journal of Consumer Research* `[unverified]`. Implemented: sticker book starts at 2 of 12; first hat and unicorn coat are gifts; guest progress persists so an account is "save what I have".
6. **Avatar customization raises identification and motivation; avatar appearance changes behaviour.** Birk et al. 2016; Turkay and Kinzer 2014; Yee and Bailenson 2007 `[all unverified; the "carries over afterwards" claim belongs to a 2009 follow-up]`. Implemented: a 60-second creator for guests, outfits visible to teammates, and the hat as a gameplay object raccoons steal and wear.
7. **Systemic comedy outlasts scripted jokes.** Practitioner accounts from Untitled Goose Game and Octodad; Dormann and Biddle 2009 `[unverified; practitioner opinion]`. Implemented: raccoon reaction states fired by triggers, unruly unicorn physics, a cargo character with face states, and only about forty walkie-talkie lines in the whole game.
8. **Failure works when it is cheap and shared.** Juul 2013, *The Art of Failure*; Lethal Company's designer on "laughing at death" `[unverified]`. Implemented: pillion instead of death, review card and firing letter, instant retry, nothing persistent lost.
9. **No loot boxes, near-misses or dark patterns.** Zendle and Cairns 2018; Drummond and Sauer 2018; Zagal, Björk and Lewis 2013 `[unverified; the first is correlational, the last is a taxonomy]`. Implemented: fixed visible prices, no randomized items, no streaks, the account prompt appears once, any future purchase has a confirm step.
10. **The first session decides retention; keep a round inside a typical mobile session.** GameAnalytics 2025 benchmarks `[unverified vendor figures for native apps; the panel found no browser-game benchmark]`. Implemented: tap-to-riding under 30 seconds, a lobby that is already a toy, 3-to-5-minute deliveries, teach-in-play first delivery.
11. **Lethal Company, R.E.P.O. and Content Warning each run on a quota, a bounded run, a shared physical burden and a faceless boss; PEAK ships less than that** `[unverified]`. A lineage pattern, not a genre law. Implemented: the Grillmaster's rising quota, three deliveries with a hard end, the cart, and "does this make the group talk?" as the gate on every mechanic.
12. **Mobile Safari closes the WebSocket when the tab is backgrounded.** Apple Developer Forums thread 696310 `[verified by the panel's checker, not re-opened by me]`. Implemented: reconnect-on-visible with a 60-second seat hold, pillion on disconnect.

Pitfalls designed around: forced accounts or reading before play; parallel co-op; competition-first framing; reflex precision as the skill gate on phones; a fixed-position virtual joystick; loot boxes and near-miss animations; appointment mechanics and invite pyramids; scripted jokes on timers; punishing failure; over-juicing without reduce-motion and shake-off toggles; empty collections; 100vh, the Fullscreen API, orientation lock and vibrate on iPhone; audio before a user gesture; Supabase Realtime or Vercel Functions as the game server; a host-elected client holding persistent rewards; trope-only cloning of the genre.

## 5. What we need from Jay on story

**Direct answer: about one page, written in one sitting, and no plot.** For this kind of game, systems and social play carry the fun and story is seasoning. Lazzaro's paper on the four keys to fun is titled "more emotion without story" `[unverified; a practitioner white paper with about thirty participants, not a study]`, the MDA framework treats narrative as one of eight kinds of fun `[unverified]`, and the three games this design descends from ship a premise paragraph, a boss voice and a quota `[unverified]`. More story does not add fun in v1 and would cost the art and voice budget that the raccoon reaction states need.

The one page from Jay should cover:

1. **Tone.** A dial from 1 (cozy) to 10 (absurd). Default 7.
2. **The poster line.** One sentence under the title. Default: "Four unicorns. One giant hot dog. Too many raccoons."
3. **The premise, or a veto of ours.** Draft: Glizzy Lovers is the volunteer catering co-op that delivers the Grand Glizzy, Frankie, to the county cookout every year. The raccoons of Trashwood Creek want the toppings. The only transport left is UniShare, a gig-economy unicorn rental. The Grillmaster, the co-op's unseen and permanently disappointed boss, sets the quota over a walkie-talkie. Default frame: a satirical bad job, because the quota, the firing letter and the PROBATION tags already assume one, and being fired from a volunteer job is the joke.
4. **Must-have characters and jokes.** Three to five names with one trait each, any real-friend cameos with permission, and 10 to 20 slapstick things Jay actually finds funny, in any form (memories, memes, video links).
5. **Anything Jay hates.** Words, gags, tones, art styles, references to avoid.

The team invents and Jay vetoes: route names and layouts, raccoon names, the Grand Glizzy's faces, the walkie-talkie lines, sticker and item names, cosmetic set themes, review and firing-letter copy, the crew-name generator, the boss's voice. Not needed for v1, and discouraged: chapters, cutscenes, dialogue trees, backstories longer than a line, a story mode. If Jay wants a storyline later, the slot the format supports is one 20-second radio message per new route in a content drop.

## 6. Pushback

Ranked by impact. Each has a proposed resolution; none is decided.

1. **Scope.** Five of six pitches came in at four to six months for one developer, and the panel's own estimates ran optimistic. Expected case for one developer with AI tooling and AI-assisted art: about five months to the v0 in the PRD, including three weeks of slack for gate failures; three to six weeks to a trustworthy verdict on whether the toy works and is funny. An artist does not shorten the code schedule, so there is no "faster with an artist" number.

2. **The whole game is one physics toy that has to feel right on phones over cellular** `[panel judgment]`. Jointly-controlled physics is the co-op case that degrades worst with lag, and the fallback (cart follows the crew's centroid) is a less funny game. The netcode model for v0 is deliberately the simplest one: the server simulates everything and sends state 20 times a second; every client renders every body, including its own unicorn, by interpolating server state with a 100 ms buffer; there is no client prediction, so a rope never has its two ends in different time frames. Input latency of roughly 150 to 250 ms at 150 ms round-trip is hidden three ways: the thumb ring, the unicorn's lean animation and the sound start instantly on input; rope particles are client-side cosmetics drawn between the two synced endpoints; and the cart is tuned heavy and damped so late motion reads as mass. Gate A in the PRD measures this with first-time testers in a blind comparison and with an on-device delay measurement. If it fails, five days on one alternative (client prediction of the rider with the rope as a soft spring, or the formation model), and if that fails, the pivot to Raccoon Rush. No content is built on an unproven toy.

3. **The name has three separate risks.** (a) "Glizzy" also means a Glock in older slang, and there are live trademark filings for "GLIZZY GUN" toys and "GLIZZY GVNG" clothing `[unverified, seen as search snippets]`. (b) "Glizzy gobbler" is a TikTok innuendo `[unverified]`, and "Glizzy Lovers" as a phrase sits in the same neighbourhood; at a 13+ default this is tolerable, at an Everyone rating it is a conversation with Jay, not a content rule. (c) National food brands now use the word in headlines `[unverified]`; the panel reads that as the slang peaking `[panel judgment]`. Resolution: the first screen shows the hot dog; zero firearm imagery or "clip" gags; no "gobbler" or eating innuendo; "Glizzy" becomes an in-world proper noun so the title survives the slang cooling; always brand as the full two words with a mascot; and before spending on a wordmark, one day of work that is not done yet: a USPTO search and a domain and handle check.

4. **Username-only accounts have no password recovery.** Supabase needs an email on a password account; a synthetic one with email confirmation switched off works, but that forfeits the built-in reset flow `[verified from Supabase docs]`. Resolution: a one-time recovery code at signup with plain copy ("lose the password and the code and the closet is gone"), optional real-email linking later, and "no recovery without the code" as a stated support policy. Also: the anonymous sign-in endpoint is rate-limited to 30 per hour per IP and Supabase strongly recommends a CAPTCHA or Turnstile challenge on it `[verified]`, so the anonymous user is created after the first delivery, never on the join tap. The room server holds delivery-one rewards until that user exists, retries at the next Topping Stop if sign-in fails or is rate-limited, and drops them with a message if it never succeeds.

5. **Rewards must not be forgeable, including in solo.** A client-run simulation reporting its own result is the reason PlayroomKit was rejected, so solo cannot run that way either. Resolution: solo runs in a server room with a bot unicorn, same code path as multiplayer, rewards server-authoritative. If the server is unreachable, an offline local simulation is playable, marked "practice", and earns nothing. The load test in the plan sizes rooms per machine with solo included.

6. **Solo is probably the most common first session and every pitch called it "practice"** `[panel judgment; no data]`. Resolution: solo earns stickers (in a server room, above), and ends on the share link with "this is funnier with three more people". The literal first 60 seconds for a solo guest is a design deliverable. Do not market solo.

7. **Silent groups.** In-browser voice on iOS Safari is unreliable `[unverified]` and the panel assumed Discord or FaceTime. Default assumption: Jay's group talks on Discord or FaceTime; the laugh test still runs with microphones muted so the game has to carry itself; tension rings, pings, the THAT button and emotes are first-class; in-browser voice is out of v1.

8. **Headcount** `[panel judgment]`. Friend groups on a call are often five or six, and no co-op pitch supports six. Resolution: test six during hardening (it costs no content, only ropes and a zoom-out); ship 2 to 6 only if the phone-readability gate passes, otherwise 2 to 4 and say so on the title screen. This is why the second must-answer question is about Jay's actual group.

9. **Guest progress is not "forever" on iPhone, and rewards on the server don't fix that by themselves.** Safari can evict script-writable storage after seven days without a visit (WebKit's tracking-prevention announcement `[unverified]`); links opened inside Discord, iMessage, Instagram or TikTok run in webviews with separate storage `[unverified]`; a site added to the iOS home screen gets storage separate from Safari `[unverified]`. The anonymous identity that owns the server-side rewards lives in that same storage, so eviction severs it. Resolution, now in v0: after the first reward the lobby shows a six-character claim code (also on the "Save your hat" card); entering it on any device or browser re-binds the anonymous identity there, and a used code rotates. The iOS install hint appears only after the player has an account or has been shown the claim code. A banner in detected in-app webviews says "open in Safari or Chrome to keep your hat".

10. **Genre churn is structural.** AppMagic, a market tracker, reports most players of these games gone within days and about 3% still playing after a month `[unverified]`. Resolution: define success as a few great evenings per friend group plus steady new groups, budget three or four named drops, and promise nobody longevity. Measure day-one return rather than targeting a native-app benchmark.

11. **Server operations for one person.** One always-on machine is a single point of failure; redeploying it kills every live room mid-delivery; a cached client can talk to a newer server after a deploy. Resolution: graceful drain on deploy (stop creating rooms, let deliveries finish, cap at ten minutes), a protocol version check with a "please refresh" message, a health check and a second machine before any public link, a "room full, try again" lobby state, and a load test of how many rooms one machine holds.

12. **Who is the host, and what about strangers?** Colyseus rooms have no built-in host concept `[unverified; from docs excerpts]`. Resolution: the room creator holds a host token stored in server room state, transferable, and passed to the longest-connected player if the host is gone for 60 seconds; settings live on the server so a host's phone going dark changes nothing. Codes are four letters from A to Z minus I and O, 24 symbols, about 330 thousand codes, which a script can enumerate, so: per-IP join rate limiting, host can lock the room, host can kick and a kicked player cannot rejoin that room, an emote and ping cooldown of one per two seconds, a blocklist for rude codes, a profanity filter on display names with unicode and leetspeak normalization that also applies to share cards, private-by-code only at launch. A report button is v1.

13. **Legal and privacy** `[unverified; not legal advice; a question for a lawyer]`. Username and password accounts plus telemetry mean a privacy policy, terms, an account-deletion flow, and analytics consent for EU visitors; collecting persistent identifiers from children raises COPPA questions in the US. Default: the game is 13+, and guest telemetry is aggregate-only with no persistent identifier until an account exists. If Jay overrides to E10+ or Everyone, that conversation happens before v0 ships, not after.

14. **iPhone audio.** The ring/silent switch mutes web audio in Safari `[unverified, widely reported]`, so the boss's walkie-talkie and the raccoon screams will be silent for many players. Resolution: subtitles for every walkie line, comedy that reads visually first, and the laugh test run once with the switch on silent. Colour-blind-safe tension rings, a photosensitivity toggle, the boost button as a hold-release alternative, and minimum tap targets are in v0 scope.

15. **Art.** The reaction states are the long pole: five raccoon archetypes with about seven animations each, six Grand Glizzy faces, the creator, unicorn coats and a route. Default: AI-assisted 2D art, disclosed wherever a store or portal asks `[unverified; copyright status of AI-generated art varies by jurisdiction]`; one raccoon rig with props and palette swaps, layered paper-doll riders, reaction states scheduled before any cosmetic set, and an art track in the plan with a weekly asset target.

16. **Discoverability.** A browser game has no store page, no ratings and no install prompt on iOS beyond a hint `[panel judgment]`; the panel's read of press coverage is that this genre spreads by clips `[unverified]`, and someone has to make the first one. Resolution: a landing page with a ten-second loop is in v0 scope; Jay's own group is the seed; Discord Activity and portals are evaluated after v0. This is not yet a marketing plan.

17. **Regions.** One server location means a cross-continent friend group gets the full round-trip. Resolution: ask where Jay's friends live (the second must-answer question); the title screen states supported regions.

## 7. Suggested stack

**Stack A (recommended): Phaser 4 client, Colyseus room server on one always-on Fly.io machine, Supabase for auth and Postgres only, static client on Vercel.** Versions `[verified against the npm registry 2026-09-30]`: Phaser 4.2.1 (4.0.0 shipped 2026-04-10), Colyseus 0.18.8. Fits because a single shared physics body with four inputs needs one authoritative simulation; Colyseus provides state patches at 20 per second, private rooms with custom four-letter IDs, seat holds for reconnection and SDK auto-reconnect `[unverified; from Colyseus docs excerpts]`. Supabase Realtime is not used in v0; Colyseus already knows who is in the room. Cost: one always-on machine at about $10 to $20 a month plus Supabase's free tier during v0; a second machine and Supabase Pro at $25 for a public launch, about $45 to $65 a month `[unverified pricing]`.

**Stack B: PixiJS 8 client, partyserver on Cloudflare Durable Objects, Supabase.** Idle rooms cost nothing with the hibernation API `[unverified]`. The arithmetic: four players at 20 updates a second is 80 messages a second; billed at roughly 20 messages per request `[unverified billing ratio]` that is about 4 requests a second, about 345 thousand a day for one room, which exhausts a 100-thousand-per-day free tier in about seven hours. So it is paid from the first evening, and you hand-roll the tick loop, state diffing and reconnection that Colyseus provides; partyserver's README still calls itself work in progress `[unverified]`.
